To run social media for several clients without chaos, you need **one calendar for all your clients, a written brand voice for each of them, a clear approval flow and fixed deadlines**. You get access to accounts through the networks' official roles, never through passwords. Once that's in place, each new client is a few hours of work a week, and no longer a source of stress.

:::ukratko
- Access to accounts goes through roles on the networks, and the accounts stay the client's.
- **Every client has their own brand voice**, written down on a single page.
- The client approves posts through one link, not through ten messages on Viber.
- Fixed deadlines: the plan by the 25th of the month, approval within 48 hours.
- The monthly report says what worked and what's changing, not just the numbers.
:::

## The biggest problems when you have several clients

The first problem is scatter. One client's posts are in a spreadsheet, another's in Canva, a third's in messages. When a client asks “what's going out next week?”, it takes you ten minutes to answer.

The second is approvals. The client gets a photo on Viber, replies three days later with “fine, just change that word”, and you don't know which word. The post goes out late, and of course it's the person running the social media who gets the blame.

The third is voice. After a month of working for five clients, they all start to sound the same: the way you write. Clients notice.

## Access to accounts: roles, not passwords

**Never ask a client for their password.** The networks have official ways to give access to another person or company: Meta business portfolio (Business Manager) for Facebook and Instagram, admin roles on a LinkedIn page (for example content admin), and users on a Google Business Profile. That way the client can always see who has access and can remove it, and you don't risk being held responsible for their account.

**The accounts always stay the client's.** If the work together ends, they keep the profile, the followers and the history, and you simply lose access. That's fair, and it protects you from misunderstandings too.

## A brand voice for every client

Your first meeting with a new client should end with a one-page document: who the brand speaks to, whether it addresses people formally or casually, three words that describe it, the words it uses and the ones it avoids, and an example of a good post. There's a six-question template in the article on [brand voice](@glas-brenda-na-drustvenim-mrezama).

Alongside the voice, write down the facts: services, prices, opening hours, addresses, and what the client never promises. When you're writing your fifth post of the day, for your third company, you won't have to remember whether the salon opens on Sundays.

## How a single post moves through the process

A good flow has five steps, and each one has an owner:

1. Monthly plan: you propose topics by the 25th of the previous month, and the client confirms them.
2. Draft: text and a visual for each network, in the calendar.
3. Approval: the client sees the posts in one place and approves them or leaves a comment.
4. Publishing: scheduled at the right time, in the client's time zone.
5. Report: once a month, with a recommendation for the following month.

The key part is step three. Send the client one link where they can see all the posts for the week or the month, with a preview of how each will look on every network. They leave their comments there, next to the post, rather than in a message with no context. Make the **approval deadline** clear, say 48 hours, and agree on what happens if the client stays silent: the post goes out or it waits.

## Deadlines that save your nerves

**Fixed deadlines** are the simplest tool that agencies neglect. Here's an example that works well:

| What | Deadline |
|---|---|
| Proposed plan for next month | by the 25th of the month |
| Client confirms the plan | by the 28th of the month |
| Drafts for the week | on Friday, for the following week |
| Draft approval | 48 hours |
| Monthly report | by the 5th of the month |

When the client knows the deadlines, they stop sending urgent messages on Sunday evening. When you know them, the work spreads out evenly. What a good monthly plan looks like is covered in the article on [the content calendar](@plan-objava-za-drustvene-mreze).

## A report the client actually reads

A client isn't interested in thirty charts. They want to know whether their social media is working and what comes next. A good report fits on one or two pages: reach and engagement compared with last month, the three best posts, the number of messages and enquiries, and one to three recommendations. Which numbers to include and which to skip is covered in the article on [social media analytics](@analitika-drustvenih-mreza).

## How many clients can one person handle?

It depends on the workload, but a rough calculation helps. A client with two networks and three posts a week takes 15 to 20 hours a month when everything is done by hand, as we worked out in the article on [how much social media management costs](@cena-vodjenja-drustvenih-mreza). Once you add meetings, messages and admin, that realistically means six to eight clients for one full-time person.

Tools that write first drafts, adapt posts for each network and schedule them can halve that time. The same person can then run more clients and the quality holds, because their time goes on what a tool can't do: talking to clients, taking photos and making decisions.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I get access to a client's accounts without their password?

Through the official roles: Meta business portfolio for Facebook and Instagram, an admin role on a LinkedIn page, and user on a Google Business Profile. That way the client keeps control and can remove your access.

### How should a client approve posts?

Ideally through one link where they see all the posts for the period and can leave a comment on each one. Agree on an approval deadline, say 48 hours, and on what happens if they don't reply.

### How many clients can one person manage?

When everything is done by hand, six to eight clients with two networks and three posts a week each. With a tool that writes first drafts and schedules posts, more is possible without a drop in quality.

### What should I put in a monthly client report?

Reach and engagement compared with last month, the three best posts, the number of messages and enquiries, and one to three recommendations for the next month. Keep it short, on one or two pages.
