A LinkedIn company page makes sense when **your customers are other companies or the people who make decisions inside them**: owners, directors, procurement managers, HR. For businesses like these, two posts a week (case studies, professional tips and stories about the team) bring in more enquiries than any other network. The biggest reach, though, doesn't come from the page itself. It comes from employees who share the posts and comment on them.

## Who really needs LinkedIn?

LinkedIn isn't for everyone. It will bring few customers to a café, a bakery or a hair salon, because people don't go there to pick a place for breakfast. But if you sell accounting, IT services, marketing, legal advice, equipment for companies or training, or if you're hiring, LinkedIn is where the decision makers are, and they're there while they're thinking about work.

The second reason to be on LinkedIn is recruitment. Candidates check out a company before the interview. A page with regular posts about the team and its projects tells them more than a job ad does.

## How to set up the page

Sort out the basics before your first post. People who land on the page decide within a few seconds whether you're serious.

1. A logo and a cover image in good quality, in your brand colours.
2. A short tagline under the name, in one sentence: what you do and for whom.
3. An About section with the words people search for: services, city, industry.
4. Website address, location, company size and sector.
5. A call-to-action button, for example a visit to your website or a contact link.
6. Connect your employees: ask them to list the company on their profiles, so the page shows up there too.

If you need a one-sentence description, use the same one you wrote down for your [brand voice](@glas-brenda-na-drustvenim-mrezama). The same sentence on your website, Instagram and LinkedIn makes you easier to recognise.

## Five types of post that work

**A case study is the strongest format for services.** The client's problem, what you did, the result in numbers. “We cut order processing time for a client in Kragujevac from three days to one.” Leave out the client's name if you don't have permission to use it.

A professional tip shows that you know your trade. A deadline companies forget, a mistake in a contract, a change in regulations explained in five sentences.

Team and culture posts show the people. A new colleague, a work anniversary, what a working day looks like. Posts like these often get the most reactions, because people react to people.

Company news: a new product, a new office, a certificate, an award. Keep it short and say why it matters to customers.

A document, meaning a carousel in PDF form, slide by slide. It works well for step-by-step tips and short guides. It's the same principle as an Instagram carousel, which is covered in the article on [carousel posts](@instagram-karusel).

## How often and when?

For a small company's page, **two posts a week** on weekdays is a good start. People scroll LinkedIn most in the morning, before and at the start of the working day, and around lunchtime. At weekends the network is quieter. As with any network, the page statistics after a month will show what holds true for your audience.

Consistency matters more than volume. Two posts a week over a whole year build a reputation. Five posts in one week followed by three months of silence build nothing. There's more about rhythm in the article on [how often to post](@koliko-cesto-objavljivati-na-drustvenim-mrezama).

## Your employees are your biggest reach

On LinkedIn, **personal profiles usually reach far more people** than company pages. **People trust a person more than a logo.** So the best strategy for a small company is a combination: the page posts, and the owner and employees share the post with a sentence of their own, or write their own posts about their work.

Don't force anyone. Ask two or three people who enjoy it. Give them a topic and the freedom to write in their own words. One honest comment from a colleague is worth more than ten identical shares.

## Tone on LinkedIn

LinkedIn calls for a more serious tone than Instagram, but that doesn't mean corporate language. Nobody reads “end-to-end solutions for process optimisation”. Everyone reads “We help companies process orders in one day instead of three”. Write the way you'd talk to a client in a meeting: clearly, specifically, without inflated words.

**The first two lines matter most**, because LinkedIn hides the rest of the text behind “more”. It's the same rule as for Instagram captions, covered in the article on [how to write Instagram captions](@kako-napisati-opis-za-instagram-objavu). Finish with two or three hashtags for your profession and sector.

## Links in posts

Many people who run LinkedIn pages notice that posts with a link to an external website do worse than posts without one, because the network wants people to stay on it. That's why a common practice is to write the text so it has value without a click, and to put the link in the first comment. LinkedIn hasn't officially confirmed this as a rule, so test it on your own page.

## How do you know LinkedIn is working?

Likes on LinkedIn say little. Look at three things: who reacts (are they people from the companies you sell to?), how many messages and enquiries come in after your posts, and how many people visit your page and your website. One enquiry from the right director is worth more than a hundred likes from people in your own trade. The page statistics also show which companies and sectors your visitors come from, which helps you see whether you're reaching the right audience. The other numbers worth tracking are described in the article on [social media analytics](@analitika-drustvenih-mreza).

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a small company need a LinkedIn page?

If your customers are other companies or you're hiring, yes. If you sell to consumers, like a café or a salon, LinkedIn isn't a priority. Your time is better spent on Instagram and your Google Business Profile.

### Which is better, a personal profile or a company page?

Both, together. The page gives you credibility and a place for information, while the personal profiles of the owner and employees bring reach and trust.

### How often should I post on a LinkedIn page?

Two posts a week, on weekdays, is a good start. Keeping the rhythm going for months matters more than posting every day.

### Should I put the link in the post or in a comment?

Many people notice that posts without an external link do better, so they put the link in the first comment. LinkedIn hasn't officially confirmed this, so compare both approaches on your own page.
